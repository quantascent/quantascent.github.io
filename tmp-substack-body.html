I created QuantAscent because I wanted to find an edge in the stock market by picking my own stocks, and I believed that edge was in a company's financial statements rather than in chart patterns or day trading. Reading filings one company at a time doesn't scale, so I built software that scores every company on the same financial measurements and buys the best scores. The way it works is:

  • You choose a universe, which is the list of stocks the software is allowed to pick from, by sector, company size, and minimum share price.
  • It scores every company in that universe on a set of financial measurements called "metrics," such as profit margins, debt levels, and growth rates.
  • It buys the highest-scoring companies.
  • It holds them until your next rebalance, then re-scores the universe, sells the companies that no longer score high enough, trims or adds to the ones that do, and buys the new top scorers.
  • You can pick the metrics yourself, or let statistical tests pick them for you and re-pick at each rebalance.
  • Before you use a strategy with real money, you can test how it would have done in the past, and you can run thousands of variations at once to compare them.

A set of rules like that is what I mean by a strategy in this post.

Before I could release this to the public and ask people to pay for it in good conscience, there was one question I had to answer:

Does scoring stocks on their financial statements actually give you an edge?

If the answer is no, an index fund beats anything I could sell and I should go do something else. If the answer is yes, I want to know which feature is doing it.

So I used the software's sweep tool to build 31,000 adaptive strategies, the kind where the statistical tests pick the metrics, across 88 different universes from random combinations of settings, and I tested every one of them from 2016 to 2026. Each strategy was compared against two benchmarks: a random grouping of stocks from the same universe, and the ETF that matched its universe.

The answer

Yes on risk-adjusted returns. On raw return, the median strategy beat 78% of the random portfolios and came out ahead in most universes. Scoring stocks on their financial statements beat picking them at random on worst fall and on return per unit of risk in the main universe, every US stock over $5, and both of those results cleared the bar I set before the run. On raw return the median strategy made 11.3% per year against 7.9% for the random portfolios. That is a higher return, but the rule I committed to needs the uncertainty range to stay clear of 50, and this one reaches down to 40, so the return edge in the main universe reads not detected. Across the 88 universes the median strategy finished ahead of random on return in 64, and in 17 of them the lead was steady enough across time to clear the bar, among them micro caps, mid caps, technology, communication services, healthcare, and energy, against the 8 that chance alone could produce. That said, the last time I checked there wasn't an ETF, or a sane person, that picks stocks at random as an investment strategy. So how did the strategies compare to their respective ETF benchmarks?

About as well as professional money managers do, and these strategies had every setting chosen at random. S&P's SPIVA report finds that 14% of actively managed large-cap funds beat the S&P 500 over the ten years to 2025. Here, 46% of the strategies finished ahead of the equal-weight S&P 500, the index that weights stocks the way the software does, with the median strategy within half a point per year of it. Against the cap-weighted S&P 500, the one those funds are measured against, 13% of the strategies finished ahead with their picks held in equal amounts, which is the professionals' rate. With the same picks weighted by market cap, the way the index and those funds are weighted, 55% finished ahead before trading costs and 47% after a one-point allowance for them, more than three times the professionals' rate. The single-sector strategies faced a harder benchmark in their own sector's ETF: 25% finished ahead of it, from 4% in communication services to 67% in energy. Why use this instead of an index fund?

I had to do some serious soul searching after seeing the first results. My hope was that the strategies would have all crushed their benchmarks, and a coin flip against the equal-weight index isn't that. Then I put the numbers next to what professional managers achieve, and the picture changed. Most active funds trail their index after fees. Strategies with every setting chosen at random, with nobody choosing a universe or a structure on purpose, kept pace with the equal-weight index and finished ahead of the cap-weighted one a little more often than not when the same picks were held at market-cap weight. Three more things matter here.

  • People don't create strategies from random inputs. Every strategy in this study had its universe, its factors, and its settings drawn at random, which is the least thoughtful way anyone could use the software. The numbers in this post are what that produced.
  • ETFs don't let you choose which individual companies you invest in, or which ones you avoid.
  • Investing in individual stocks lets you diversify across sectors, market caps, share prices, and filters in any combination you want. For example, see if you can find an ETF that holds technology, energy, healthcare, and basic materials companies, where every company has a market cap over $200 million, a share price over $10, and a positive free cash flow yield. You can't. I've checked.

Who should use this software

QuantAscent is for people who want to own individual companies and want rules, not a hunch or a tip, deciding which ones. You decide the universe, the software scores every company in it on its financial statements and keeps the portfolio current at each rebalance, and you can test any idea against the past before you put money into it. The software is a poor fit if what you want is the market's return with no work, because an index fund gives you that.

If you'd rather just buy an index fund, you can stop reading here. But if you want to create your own strategies and have complete control over the stocks you invest in, keep reading to learn how I tested this idea, see the results in detail, and find out which settings produced the higher returns.

How I tested it

"Does it beat the market?" is the wrong first question. A portfolio of ten small companies and a cap-weighted index of the 500 largest companies aren't comparable, and the last decade was unusually good for the largest companies. So the first question was narrower: does the software pick better stocks than random chance would, from the same list?

That list is the universe. For every strategy, I drew 500 random portfolios from its universe, holding the same number of stocks the strategy held and redrawn every quarter, and counted how many of them the strategy beat. That count is its score. A strategy with no edge scores around 50. A strategy that beat 78% of the random portfolios scores 78.

I scored three things, because they don't move together: return, as CAGR; risk, as the worst peak-to-trough drawdown and separately as volatility; and return per unit of risk, as the Sharpe ratio. A method that makes the same return with half the drawdown has an edge. A method that makes 1% more with twice the drawdown doesn't.

The study ran on 88 universes so the answer wouldn't depend on any one choice: all eleven sectors together, each sector on its own, four sector groups, nine market-cap ranges from micro caps alone to all sizes together, and three filters, which were none, profitable companies only, or companies not in financial distress. Every universe kept to stocks trading above $5 on the day, as traded. One universe was chosen in advance as the main result: all sectors, all sizes, no filter. For each universe the sweep tool drew the settings at random, 500 times for the main universes and 200 times for the sector universes, and ran each draw as a complete strategy over the full ten years.

Beating random would show the software did something, but not which feature did it. So the main universes got four versions, run on the same stocks over the same quarters:

  1. Random picks used no financial data at all. This version scores 50 by definition, because it is the 500 random portfolios the others were measured against.
  2. Library only scored companies on the software's metric library, one metric per factor chosen at random, with no statistical test. This stands in for the Static Builder, where you choose the metrics yourself.
  3. Adaptive, any settings added the statistical test that chooses the metrics for you, with every setting sampled at random.
  4. Adaptive, default settings was the same thing on the default settings and the preset structures the app ships with, which it calls templates. This is the product as a new user first sees it.

A few terms come up in the results. The library's 77 metrics are grouped into 19 factors, such as margins, cash flow yields, valuation multiples, debt ratios, growth rates, and momentum, and each metric has a direction the textbook says it should work in: a higher return on capital is better, a higher debt-to-equity ratio is worse. A structure is the list of factors in a strategy and the weight on each. In an adaptive strategy you still choose the structure and how many companies to hold. The statistical test chooses the specific metric inside each factor and re-chooses it every quarter, using only data from before that date. If too few factors have a metric that qualifies, the strategy holds cash for the quarter.

The pass and fail thresholds were written down and committed to the repo before anything ran. EDGE meant the median strategy scored at least 60 with the uncertainty range clear of 50. HARM meant 40 or below with the range clear of 50. The range is a 95% range from a bootstrap, which means the ten years were re-drawn a thousand times, four consecutive quarters at a time in a random order with repeats allowed, every strategy was re-scored on each re-drawn decade, and the range is where the score landed 95% of the time. It's wide, because ten years is only 41 quarters. Everything in between was NOT DETECTED, because ten years of quarterly data can't tell a small edge from none. With 88 universes, about four would pass by luck alone if the method did nothing, so a broad claim needed more than eight.

The data covered about 6,700 US companies from 2009 to mid-2026, including roughly 2,000 that no longer exist, with the portfolio taking the loss when one was delisted mid-quarter. The ten years from August 2016 to July 2026 were scored. Every pick in this study was out of sample. At each rebalance, the statistical test used only data from before that date, and the following quarter's return was something it had never seen. The one thing that wasn't out of sample is the library itself. I chose which 77 metrics are in it, and which direction each one points, during the same years the study scores. The directions are the textbook ones, so there was little to fit, but it's still a bias in the software's favor that I can't remove. So every setting was drawn at random instead of chosen by me, and version 2 shows the library on its own, so you can see how much of the result rests on it.

The metric library did most of the work

The library is the heart of the software. It's the 77 financial-statement metrics QuantAscent computes for every US company every quarter, each with the direction the textbook says it should work in, grouped into 19 factors. Every strategy you build, static or adaptive, scores companies on it. Version 2 used nothing else: one metric per factor, chosen at random, no statistical test. It scored 83 on return, 86 on drawdown, and 93 on Sharpe, and all three cleared the bar.

Table 3. The scores from Figure 1 with their 95% ranges, written low to high, and the number of strategies behind each row. Every score in rows 2 to 4 met the EDGE threshold except the return score of version 3, marked n.d. for not detected, whose range reaches 40.

The random portfolios were a real opponent. The main universe was about 3,300 companies per quarter, micro caps included. Owned in equal amounts, that whole universe compounded at 8.7% per year over the ten years, and the micro-cap portion alone made 4.4%. A random ten-stock portfolio from that list was not a basket of failing companies, and the median one made 7.9% per year. That is the comparison every score in this post is measured against.

In return terms, the median strategy in every version made 11% to 12% per year while the random portfolios made 8%. Scoring companies on the library was worth about three to four points of annual return over random stock picking, and about seven points of worst fall, and it didn't matter much which metric from a factor did the scoring. The return gap is the part the pre-set rule couldn't confirm in the main universe, because a three-point gap over ten years sits inside the range that luck can produce. The risk gap is the part it could. Both come from the part of the software every user runs, whether they build their strategies by hand or let the statistical test build them.

The statistical test cut volatility, and the default settings cut the bad tail

Switching the test on, with everything else held fixed, moved the typical strategy a little and the spread of strategies more. Read left to right across versions 1 to 4, the return scores were 50 / 83 / 78 / 82, the drawdown scores 50 / 86 / 88 / 82, and the Sharpe scores 50 / 93 / 94 / 95. None of the steps between versions met the 5-point threshold I had set for "the test adds something," so every step read NOT DETECTED. The other place to look is the median strategy's actual return, drawdown, and volatility (Figure 2). The test took the median return from 12.0% to 11.3%, the worst fall from 27% to 25%, and volatility from 19% to 16%, and the volatility step was the one whose range stayed clear of zero.

Figure 2. What each step changed in the median strategy, with its 95% range. The top row of each panel is the statistical test being switched on, and the bottom row is the default settings. The test cost under a point of return, made the worst fall two points shallower, and cut volatility by two and a half points, the one change whose range stayed clear of zero.

The flat return is what the design predicts. The statistical test decides which metric stands in for each factor, for example which of the value metrics a strategy uses that quarter. It never decides which factors the strategy uses, and two metrics from the same factor rank companies in much the same order, so swapping one for another moves the return very little. The test's job is risk, and that is where it showed up.

Those numbers mix every kind of strategy the sweep built, including single-factor strategies and five-stock portfolios. A fairer test is to compare like with like: the same kind of strategy, blends of two or more factors holding 8 to 12 stocks, with the statistical test off and then on. Table 4 does that. At the same structures the test cost about a point of median return and cut the share of strategies with a 40%-or-worse drawdown from 12% to 10%, and the default settings cut it to 7%. Its stricter settings pushed the same way: strict stability, the universe floor, and a higher signal floor each traded a few points of return score for fewer deep drawdowns. (Those settings are described in the method details near the end.)

Table 4. Test off against test on at the same structures in the main universe. The last column is the share of strategies that beat RSP, the equal-weight S&P 500 ETF.

The other thing the test did was refuse to trade. Eight strategies in version 3 never held a single stock in ten years, all of them on the pure "financial strength" template, because none of its metrics ever met the floor. Version 2 traded them. Across every structure, the share of strategies that lost to random was about the same with the test on or off, 18% against 19%, while the share making under 5% per year went from 9% to 8% and the share with a 40%-or-worse drawdown from 18% to 14%. The default settings did more on every count: 10% lost to random, 3% made under 5% per year, and 11% had a 40%-or-worse drawdown.

Both versions climb toward 100, with the library-only version holding the taller stack at the top, and both carry a thin tail at the far left. The adaptive version's extra strategies sit between 45 and 60.

The statistical test didn't lift the typical strategy. The default settings trimmed the worst ones. 5% of version 2's strategies scored below the 15th percentile and 5% of version 3's below the 14th. 5% of version 4's scored below the 37th.

Backtests predicted how a strategy behaves, not what it returns

Choose a date and treat it as the present. Every strategy's record up to that date is its backtest, and the two years after it are its future. Rank the strategies by backtest, rank them by future, and check whether the two rankings agree. I did this at four dates.

Table 6. Whether a strategy's backtest predicted its next two years. A rank correlation of +1 would mean the backtest ranking held exactly, and 0 would mean it carried no information. Return reached the +0.20 threshold at one date of four. Drawdown reached it at two, the two most recent.

Figure 4. The same test with one dot per strategy, its backtest CAGR across the bottom and its CAGR over the next two years up the side. Only the first window has a visible slope. In the other three, a better backtest did not mean a better future.

Four dates is a thin test, so after the run I repeated it at every quarter, 33 dates from August 2018 on, with the next quarter alone as the future. The rank correlation for return averaged +0.01 with a standard error of 0.03, and ranking by Sharpe instead, or keeping only the diversified strategies with at least two factor families and ten or more holdings, gave the same answer. That settles the return side at four dates and at thirty-three: a strategy's past return doesn't rank it against the other strategies in the family. The useful question is what a backtest does carry forward, so I checked every property it reports the same way, against the next two years at the four dates. Table 7 ranks them.

Table 7. Which properties of a strategy's backtest persisted into its next two years, at the four dates of Table 6, ranked by the mean rank correlation. Turnover is the share of holdings replaced at each rebalance, tracking error is how much the strategy's quarterly return differed from SPY's, and the stood-aside share is the fraction of quarters the strategy held cash. n/a marks a date where no quarter fell in one of the two windows. The quarterly test and this table were added after the run and carry no verdict.

The top of that table is the part of a backtest you can act on, and it's what the software is built around. How much a strategy trades, how often it stands aside, how far its returns differ from the index's, and how much it swings all carried forward into the next two years. The sweep tool lists every run with its worst fall, its Sharpe, and its standing against luck, and the backtest page reports its volatility and its turnover, and Table 7 says the trading and the swings are the numbers that will still describe the strategy two years later. The worst fall carried forward at the two most recent dates and not at the first two, so decide the amount of trading and the size of swings you can live with, and choose a strategy whose record stays within those limits. The return side is settled at the level of the method rather than the single run: scored picks beat random picks on drawdown in every two-year window and on return in two of the four (Figure 6), so every strategy that clears the luck cutoff draws on that, and the risk you choose decides how smoothly it gets there.

Return DID NOT PERSIST, and drawdown DID NOT PERSIST by the rule either, at two dates of four. If you had taken the top 10% of strategies by backtest return at each date and followed them for the next two years, they finished ahead of the strategies they had outranked at two of the four dates, level at one, and behind at one. This is why the sweep tool doesn't rank strategies by return. It's for seeing how a whole range of settings behaved, not for choosing a winner.

The app also shows every sweep run against a luck cutoff, which it calls the luck bar, and tells you two things: discard anything that doesn't clear it, and prefer the lower-risk runs. I applied both rules mechanically at each date and checked the next two years. Neither met the 5-point threshold at three of four dates, though the lower-drawdown runs did keep a shallower drawdown at three of the four, by three to four points, just not strongly enough to count. The luck cutoff stays anyway, because its job is to stop you from treating a lucky run as a good one, not to find winners.

It kept pace with the S&P 500

The product holds every pick in equal amounts, so the fair index to compare it with is RSP, the equal-weight S&P 500 ETF, which made 11.3% per year over the ten years. The median strategy on the default settings made 11.8%, and the median sweep strategy made 11.0%. Depending on the version, 46% to 59% of the strategies finished ahead of RSP. By the rule I set before the run, which needed 60% or more ahead with the uncertainty range clear of 50%, that's a tie. In plain terms, the typical strategy did about what the equal-weight index did, and it did it with settings a random number generator chose. S&P's SPIVA report finds that about 14% of actively managed large-cap funds beat the S&P 500 over the ten years to 2025, after fees. Half of these strategies kept pace with the equal-weight index before anyone had chosen anything on purpose.

The comparison everyone asks about is against the regular cap-weighted S&P 500, and SPY made 15.0% per year by holding the giant companies in giant size. With the picks held in equal amounts, 13% to 27% of the strategies beat it, which is the professionals' rate. So as a side calculation I re-weighted the same picks by market cap and compared them with SPY. That brought the median strategy to 15.6% per year, and 55% of them finished ahead of SPY (Figure 5). In the large-cap universes, the ones a large-cap fund would actually hold, 52% to 67% finished ahead, with the median strategy 0.3 to 2.9 points per year ahead of SPY. The professionals' 14% is after fees, so take a point off every strategy for trading costs, the estimate in the cost paragraph below: 45% to 62% still finished ahead. So why does Table 8 still say TIES for that row? Because SPY's 15% came from a few enormous quarters, and the test asks whether the result would hold in a different ten years. The bootstrap, the same one behind every range in this post, re-drew the decade a thousand times. In the draws that included those big quarters twice, 29% of the strategies beat SPY. In the draws that missed them, 74% did. That range includes 50%, so the rule calls it a tie. The 55% is real for the decade that happened. Whether it was the method or the decade's mix of quarters, ten years can't say.

Cap weighting was also where the statistical test mattered most. Switching it on moved the cap-weighted median from 13.2% to 15.6% per year and the share beating SPY from 37% to 55%. The ranges on those steps reached below zero, and the cap-weighted comparison was registered as secondary before the run, so it can't carry a verdict on its own. It's the first thing I'd test next. The product holds every pick in equal amounts today. I'm considering a market-cap weighting option for a future release, which would make this comparison the product's own number instead of a side calculation.

Two caveats on this section. Dead-company coverage in the data is a handful of companies per year before 2016, which is why the scoring starts there, and only partial from 2016 to 2020. Missing dead companies raise the software's returns but not the index's, so this comparison leans in the software's favor. Trading isn't free, and none of the numbers in this post include its cost. The typical strategy sold and replaced about seven of its ten stocks every quarter, which comes to roughly 60 trades a year. Using the app's default assumptions of $1 per trade plus a small price concession on each one, that costs about 1% of a $10,000 account per year, and about two thirds of 1% on a $100,000 account. So knock up to a point off every return in this post, depending on your account size.

Two more costs the study doesn't model, because they depend on your account. The subscription is $171 a year, which is 1.7% of a $10,000 account and 0.17% of $100,000. On a $10,000 account the software plus its trading comes to about 2.7% a year that an index fund doesn't charge, so the typical strategy, which landed 0.4 points behind the equal-weight index before costs, would trail it by about three points after them. On $100,000 the two together are under 1%. Taxes are the larger cost in a taxable account. Replacing seven of ten stocks every quarter means nearly every gain is realized within a year and taxed as ordinary income. At a 24% federal rate that is roughly 2.5 points of an 11% year, where an index ETF taxes you on its dividends and defers the rest until you sell, at the lower long-term rate. Actively managed mutual funds pass their managers' trading on to you the same way, through yearly capital-gains distributions, so a fund investor in a taxable account pays this cost too; it isn't in the 14% figure, which is before the investor's taxes. In an IRA or a 401(k) the tax difference disappears.

For completeness, every version also finished ahead of owning every stock in the universe in equal amounts, which made 8.7% per year, 75% to 87% of the time. The ranges on those shares reach below 50%, because a few quarters decide how the universe average compares, so by the rule all three read TIES rather than BEATS.

Three results in Figure 7 were worth the whole study to me.

Filtering to profitable companies first showed what the library does on its own. Every size band was run a second time with the universe limited to companies with positive net income. That filter lifted the all-cap universe's average return from 8.7% per year to 10.7%, because avoiding unprofitable companies is the first thing the library does without being told to. The scored strategies landed in the same place either way, 11% to 14% per year with the filter and 9% to 16% without it, so they had already captured most of that on their own. Among profitable companies they kept a return score above 75 in three of the five bands: the median strategy beat 89% of random profitable portfolios in large caps, 82% in mid caps, and 78% in micro caps, and 58%, 50%, and 66% of them finished ahead of the equal-weight S&P 500. In small caps the edge went away, with a score of 33, and in the all-cap universe it shrank to 61. Each of those is one universe with a wide range, so none of the five met the EDGE rule on return; the Sharpe score stayed above 50 in all five bands and the drawdown score in four. As a side calculation added after the run, a cap-weighted quality ETF, QUAL, made 14.1% per year over the same quarters, and the scored picks among profitable large caps weighted by market cap made 16.0% and finished ahead of it 61% of the time. If you only want profitable companies, the library gets you most of the way there on its own, and filtering the universe first is a setting you can add in the software.

Large caps showed no risk edge. The drawdown scores were 35 to 40 across the three filters. Random portfolios of large companies were already diversified and solvent, and the software's picks had drawdowns about as deep as theirs.

Micro caps showed the biggest edge, and they're the ones that can't absorb much money. Micro caps scored 97 to 100 on everything with no filter and with the solvency filter, with the median strategy making 16% per year against 4.4% for the micro-cap universe as a whole. Small caps kept a risk edge, 87 on drawdown and 81 on Sharpe, with a return score of 62 that didn't clear the bar.

What it means if you use the software

The library is the core product. Scoring companies on its metrics in their textbook directions, with no test choosing among them, took the median strategy from 8% per year to 12% and to the 83rd percentile against random in the main universe, with a worst fall seven points shallower, and all three of its scores cleared the bar. With the statistical test added, the scoring cleared the strict threshold on return in 17 of the 88 universes against the 8 that chance could produce, and on drawdown and Sharpe in 30. That scoring runs inside every strategy you build, in the Static Builder as much as in adaptive strategies. It's the core of QuantAscent: the metric set, the directions, the financial-statement data for every US company, and the re-scoring at every rebalance, in one place.

With random settings it kept pace with the market. Half the strategies in the main universe finished ahead of the equal-weight S&P 500, and 55% finished ahead of SPY when the same picks were weighted by market cap, before any user had chosen a universe or a structure on purpose. Most professional funds don't manage that against their index after fees.

Adaptive strategies did their work on volatility, not on return. The statistical test cut the median strategy's volatility from 19% to 16%, made its worst fall two points shallower, and cost under a point of return, and it refused to run factors with no signal. The default settings trimmed the bad tail further. It didn't find return, and in this design it can't, because it chooses within factors rather than between them.

Strategies that blended factors and held ten or more companies did close to a point a year better. The gap over a single factor and five holdings was most of a point of annual return and about ten points of score. The product lets you choose it everywhere, and the defaults already do.

The other settings moved the result two to six points of score. The ones that tighten the test trade return for fewer deep drawdowns, and the longest training window did best.

The backtest tells you how a strategy behaves, and that holds. How much a strategy trades, how often it stands aside, how far it strays from the index, and how much it swings all carried forward into the next two years, at four dates and at thirty-three. Its worst fall carried forward at the two most recent dates. Past return didn't rank the strategies, so the app lists every run with its worst fall, its Sharpe, and its standing against luck instead of ranking them by return. Use the backtest to choose how much risk you take, not how much you'll make.

Method details

How the 88 universes were built. A universe came from three choices: which sectors, which company sizes, and whether to apply a filter. The size bands were micro caps under $300 million, small caps from $300 million to $2 billion, mid caps from $2 billion to $10 billion, and large caps at $10 billion and up with no upper limit, so the mega caps were in the large band. The main set crossed every size range with every filter on all sectors, which made 27 universes, plus one reference universe with the textbook distress threshold. The sector set took each sector and sector group at two sizes, with and without the profitability filter, which made 60 universes. The minimum share price was $5 throughout, measured as the price the stock traded at on the rebalance date.

The correction in this version. The first version of this article, published on October 20, applied that $5 floor to the data vendor's split-adjusted price, which is the price series the software's backtests read at the time. A split-adjusted series rewrites a company's whole history every time it splits. A company that later did a one-for-ten reverse split carried a price ten times too high on every earlier date, so it passed the floor on days it traded at fifty cents, and a company that later split forward carried a price too low and was kept out. The floor was the same on every side of every comparison, so it never favored the software over the random portfolios, but it let about 16,000 stock-quarters that traded under $5 into the universes, and those companies weighed on the random portfolios far more than on the scored strategies, which rarely picked them. The software fixed the same bug on October 21, and once the database carried the as-traded prices I re-ran the whole study on them: the same panel with one column added, the same engine plus that one fix, the same 88 universes, seeds, random portfolios, bootstrap, and thresholds. The random portfolios' typical return went from 2% to 8% per year and their worst fall from 47% to 34%. The median strategy's return moved by less than half a point in every version. Every score in this article is from the re-run. The one other change is that the library-only version's random metric choices are now seeded per strategy rather than per universe, which I found in the same audit; strategies that shared a template had been sharing one sequence of metric draws. The scorecard records all of this as Amendment 3, dated after the run, and the first version of the article stays published beside this one.

How the settings were sampled. For each universe, the settings were drawn at random from every factor, blends of one to six factors, 5 to 15 holdings, every training window and re-choosing frequency the app offers, both stability options, signal floors of 0.01, 0.02, and 0.03, and the universe floor on or off. The app allows more in places, and the study's ranges were chosen to cover what a typical user would set. Samples that came out identical were merged, which is why some universes show 494 to 500 runs rather than exactly 500. The study covered only the library's own directions. Reversing a direction is allowed in the software, and the study did not grade it.

What an adaptive strategy's settings are. The training window is all available history or the trailing 12, 16, 20, or 24 quarters. The test re-chooses its metrics every quarter or about every two years. The signal floor is the minimum average strength a metric needs to qualify. The stability setting decides whether each half of the training window must meet the floor on its own. The universe floor, when on, lets the test remove the weaker half of the universe before scoring, using the one metric whose top and bottom halves had the biggest gap in returns over the training window. All of them ship with defaults, and the exact rules are in the math section.

How the random portfolios were drawn. Each random portfolio held the same number of companies the strategy actually held that quarter, and was redrawn every quarter from the strategy's own universe. The 7.9% in Table 2 is the median over the 246,000 random portfolios the strategies were graded against. It's below the 8.7% universe average because a ten-stock random portfolio has the same average return as the universe but much more variation, and the median outcome of a volatile portfolio is below its mean.

How the backtest-predicts-future test was set up. The four dates had futures ending May 2020, May 2022, April 2024, and July 2026. The threshold was a rank correlation of +0.20 or better at three of the four dates. The app's two pieces of advice, discard anything below the luck cutoff and prefer the lower-risk runs, were applied mechanically at the same four dates, with a 5-point lead at three of four dates as the threshold. The quarterly version and Table 7 were added after the run. In both, the backtest is the record from August 2016 up to the date, with at least eight quarters behind it, on traded quarters as everywhere else, and strategies with fewer than four traded quarters in either window are left out. Tracking error is the annualized standard deviation of the strategy's quarterly return minus SPY's, turnover is the share of holdings replaced at each rebalance averaged over the window, and beta and alpha come from the regression of quarterly returns on SPY's with the risk-free rate at zero.

The data. About 6,700 US companies, 3,000 to 5,000 in any given quarter, quarterly from 2009 to mid-2026, from a commercial fundamentals vendor. The scored quarters were August 2016 through July 2026, 41 in all, and 2009 to 2016 was training history only. The roughly 2,000 dead companies were found through exchange delisting filings. Coverage of those delistings was dense from 2021 on, partial from 2016 to 2018, thin in 2019 and 2020, and essentially absent before 2016, which is why nothing before 2016 was scored. That gap, which I call the survivorship gap elsewhere in this post, raised the returns of the software, the random portfolios, and the universe average equally, so the comparison against random was clean. It raised the software's returns against the market, and I say so where it matters. Companies trading under $1 today were missing entirely, history included, and both sides shared that gap.

Costs. Every number was before trading costs and taxes. The app's backtester applies a cost model, but the records of adaptive strategies come from the walk-forward engine the study ran, which the app's own record cards label as gross. Turnover was measured so the cost could be estimated, and the estimate is in the market section above.

Conclusion

I started this with one question: does scoring stocks on their financial statements give you an edge? After 31,000 strategies on 88 universes over ten years, the answer is yes on risk against random stock picking, yes on return in a fifth of the universes, not proven on return in the main universe, and about even against the index that weights the way the product does.

The edge comes from the library. Scoring companies on the metrics in their textbook directions took the typical strategy from 8% per year to 12% in the main universe with a worst fall seven points shallower, held on drawdown in every two-year window, and cleared the strict threshold on return in 17 of the 88 universes against the 8 that chance could produce. It did that whether or not the statistical test was switched on. The test earned its place a different way: it cut volatility, with a range clear of zero, at a cost of under a point of return, and the default settings trimmed the deep drawdowns. Diversification was worth close to a point a year on top. And a strategy's backtest predicted how it would trade and how much it would swing, not what it would return, which is why the software compares settings instead of ranking winners.

Against the market, the typical strategy kept pace with the equal-weight S&P 500, which is more than most professional funds manage against their index, and it did it with settings chosen at random. The same picks weighted by market cap finished ahead of SPY in this decade a little more often than not, about three times as often as those funds did, and whether that was the method or the decade is the first thing I'd test next.

None of this is a promise about the next ten years. It's a record of what the software did over the last ten, with the thresholds set before the run and the parts that didn't pass reported next to the parts that did, including the part I had to correct.

Am I launching? Yes. If the scoring had done no better than random picking, I would have shut this down. It did better on every measure in the main universe, it cleared the bar I set on risk, and I can point to the part of the software that did it. But beating random isn't the point of the product. The point is that you can define a universe no ETF offers, with the sectors, company sizes, price floor, and filters in whatever combination you want, decide which companies you're willing to own, and test the idea against the past before you put money in it. What this study adds is that, across the 88 universes I tried, the scoring picked stocks from that list with fewer deep drawdowns than chance would have, and in a fifth of them with higher returns too. That's the product I'm launching.
