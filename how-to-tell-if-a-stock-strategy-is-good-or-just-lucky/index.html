How to tell if a stock strategy is good or just lucky

Picking stocks at random produced a ten-year record that looked like a great strategy. Here's the test I use to tell luck from a real result, and what happened when I put my own strategies through it.

Brandon Wilson · November 9, 2026 · Watch the video

This post is the written version of the video above, with a few numbers the video didn't have time for.

Would you put money into a stock strategy that returned 25.2% a year for ten years?

From August 2016 to July 2026, it turned $100,000 into $1,004,655. The S&P 500 turned the same $100,000 into $418,998, so the strategy finished 10.2 percentage points a year ahead of it. Its worst fall from a quarter-end high was 12.3%, against 15.0% for the S&P 500.

Figure 1. The strategy's record against the S&P 500, August 2016 to July 2026, before trading costs and taxes.

Now, what if I told you that the strategy picked its stocks at random?

Every 90 days, I gave a number to every US stock trading above $5 at the time, which was more than 3,000 stocks. I drew ten random numbers and bought the ten stocks they landed on, in equal amounts. Ninety days later I sold them, drew ten new numbers, and bought those instead. I did that 41 times, once a quarter for ten years, on real historical stock prices. Nothing about the companies went into the picks.

Do you still want to put your money into it? I'm guessing you don't.

Every result in this post is a hypothetical backtest, before trading costs and taxes. None of it is investment advice, and past or simulated results don't predict future ones.

I ran it 500 times

You might be wondering how picking stocks at random every quarter produced a record like that. The answer is that I didn't run the random picking once. I ran it 500 times, each time with different random numbers, and showed you the best one.

Every run drew different stocks, so every run ended up somewhere different. A few lost money, most landed somewhere in the middle, and a few did very well: 16 of the 500 lost money over the ten years, and 26 finished ahead of the S&P 500. That spread is called variance, and a portfolio of ten randomly picked stocks has a lot of it. If you run something with that much variance 500 times, one of the runs will end up far out at the top. It isn't there because it found anything special. It's there because I kept running it until one of them got lucky. Showing you only that one is called selection bias.

Figure 2. All 500 random portfolios, $100,000 each. The orange line is the record from Figure 1, and the gray area holds the middle 90% of the runs.

If I take each of those portfolios' return per year, put it into a 1-point bin, and count how many land in each bin, I get the distribution of returns. That chart is called a histogram. The median random portfolio returned 7.5% a year. The worst lost 3.3% a year, and the best was the 25.2% I opened with. The S&P 500 returned 15.0% a year, well to the right of the median.

Figure 3. Return per year of the 500 random ten-stock portfolios, August 2016 to July 2026.

So ask yourself this. The S&P 500 is a group of stocks too. Did it return more than the median random portfolio by pure luck, or is there something about that group of stocks that made it do better?

How a company gets into the S&P 500

The answer is a bit of both, because the S&P 500 isn't a random list. Under S&P Dow Jones Indices' rules, a company has to be a US company with its primary listing on the NYSE, Nasdaq or Cboe, and as of July 2025 it had to be worth at least $22.7 billion to be added. It has to have made a profit in its most recent quarter and over its last four quarters combined, have enough of its shares held by the public and traded often enough, and have been public for at least a year. Then a committee picks from the companies that qualify and keeps the sectors roughly in line with the whole market.

So the S&P 500 is a list of stocks built by rules, and the two rules that matter most are size and profit. To see what that list was worth on its own, I ran the same random draw again, ten stocks every 90 days and 500 times over, but drawing only from the companies that were in the S&P 500 on each date.

Figure 4. Return per year of 500 random ten-stock portfolios from each list: every stock (gray) and the S&P 500's members at the time (blue).

The whole distribution shifted to the right. The median random portfolio drawn from the S&P 500's members returned 10.8% a year, against 7.5% for one drawn from every stock, so the S&P's list moved the median up by 3.2 points a year. That shift is called lift. Over these ten years, a portfolio picked at random from the S&P 500's list had a higher return, on average, than one picked at random from the whole market.

Can you build your own list?

Is it possible to pick your own group of stocks in a way that shifts the distribution to the right? The answer is yes. If it weren't, fund managers wouldn't exist, and everyone's retirement account would be dictated by a random number generator.

So how do you pick stocks in a way that not only results in higher returns, but also reduces the risk that comes with that much variance? There are a lot of ways to do it, and there's no right answer for everyone. The way I focus on is building strategies from company financial statements, using statistical tests, with the goal of a higher return for the risk taken than random picking.

But think back to the start of this post. A random strategy produced 25.2% a year. If random picking can produce a record like that, how do you tell whether a strategy built on rules actually did better than random picking?

Three checks

I use three:

  1. Is the median return of the strategies higher than the median return of random picking?
  2. Did the whole distribution of strategies shift to the right of the random portfolios, and is it smooth? By smooth I mean it isn't chaotic, and its middle sits near its center, so the gain didn't come from a few outliers.
  3. Do the first two still hold during a holdout period?

Testing on data the strategy never saw

A holdout period is data the strategy had no knowledge of when it was built. That might sound obvious, because why would you test a strategy on a future it already knows? But it's an easy mistake to make with financial data. You need some data to find the patterns and other data to check them, and a strategy tested on the same data it learned from will look better than it really is.

The process I use is called walk-forward testing. Pick a date, say August 2016. The strategy learns its rules from everything before that date, picks its stocks, and holds them for 90 days. Then it moves forward 90 days and does it again: it learns from everything before the new date, picks, and holds. Every quarter's result comes from rules that had never seen that quarter, and chaining the quarters together gives the full record. The last two years are kept sealed. The record is computed on them but isn't shown until the strategy is final, so nothing can be tuned against them.

Figure 5. One strategy's record built one quarter at a time. The hatched last two years are computed but not shown until the strategy is final.

How I built the strategies

To build the strategies, I used software I make called QuantAscent. It applies statistical tests to financial data to find which metrics went along with higher returns. It ranks stocks by the metrics you choose, using those tests, and turns your rules into a list of stocks. Then it simulates trading that list over time, walk-forward. You don't need the software to use anything in this post, so instead of a walkthrough, here's how it works.

You pick a universe, which is the list of stocks it's allowed to choose from. For this test it was every US stock above $5 a share. Then you pick the families of financial metrics it can use, such as quality, growth, momentum and value.

Every quarter, using only the data that existed before that date, the software tests every metric in every family. The test is simple. It sorts the companies by the metric, then checks whether the ones that ranked higher went on to earn more over the following 90 days. It does that on every past date in the training window and averages the result. A metric qualifies only if that result was stable and above a minimum threshold.

Figure 6. The test for one metric on August 31, 2016: companies ranked by revenue growth into ten groups, and each group's average return over the next 90 days, averaged over the 28 quarters before that date. The groups didn't line up with the ranking, so revenue growth wasn't used that quarter.

The strongest qualifying metrics are combined into a score for every stock in the universe. The backtest buys the top-ranked stocks and holds them for a set period, 90 days by default, then steps forward in time and repeats the process.

Every record the software produces is compared against 500 random portfolios drawn from the strategy's own list of stocks, holding the same number of stocks over the same dates. That's the gray histogram from earlier, and the software draws it for every strategy.

Figure 7. One strategy's record against its 500 random portfolios. It returned 11.4% a year and did better than 430 of them.

It doesn't stop at one strategy, either. A sweep runs hundreds of strategies with different settings. The one in this post is 492 strategies on every US stock above $5, each holding between 5 and 15 stocks, with their settings chosen at random. It's the main universe from the edge study I published in October, re-run on the newest data, which also added two metrics to the library, so a few numbers here differ slightly from that post.

Did the strategies beat random picking?

Here's the sweep against random portfolios, with each random portfolio matched to one strategy's number of stocks and drawn from the same list.

Figure 8. Return per year of the 492 strategies (green) against 492 random portfolios from the same list, each holding the same number of stocks as its strategy (gray), August 2016 to July 2026.

On the first check, the median strategy returned 11.4% a year, against 7.3% for the random portfolios, a gap of 4.1 points a year. The random median is 7.3% here instead of the 7.5% from earlier because these random portfolios hold between 5 and 15 stocks, like the strategies they're matched to, instead of always ten.

On the second check, the whole group moved, not a few outliers. The strategies were spread out a little less than the random portfolios, with a standard deviation of 4.3 points a year against 4.7.

On the third check, the gap held in the sealed years. Over the first eight years, from 2016 to 2024, the median strategy returned 10.8% a year against 7.4% for the median random portfolio, and 75% of the strategies beat that random median. Over the last two years, the sealed ones, it was 13.0% against 7.7%, and 76% of the strategies beat it.

Why the S&P 500 beat its own members

There's one thing in Figure 4 I haven't explained yet. The S&P 500 itself returned 15.0% a year, 4.2 points more than the median random portfolio drawn from its own members. What's up with that?

The S&P 500 doesn't hold an equal amount of each company. It holds each company in proportion to how much the company is worth, so the largest companies make up most of the index. The equal-weight S&P 500 fund (RSP), which holds the same companies in equal amounts, returned 11.3% a year. So holding the same companies by size added 3.7 points a year, because over these ten years the largest companies did better than the rest.

Figure 9. The same chart as Figure 4, with the equal-weight S&P 500 (purple) and the S&P 500 (white). The gap between them is the same companies held by size instead of in equal amounts.

The difference in returns between small and large companies is called the size factor. The textbook finding is that small companies have done better over the long run, but over these ten years it went the other way.

Weighting the strategies by size

So I ran one more test on the same sweep. I kept every strategy's picks exactly as they were and changed only how much of each stock it held: each quarter, in proportion to the company's market value on the day it was bought, the way the S&P 500 does it. The random portfolios stayed in equal amounts. This test and the 30-stock test below are side calculations I added after the sweep was scored, not part of the study's original tests.

Figure 10. Return per year of the 492 strategies weighted by company size (green bars) and in equal amounts (dashed outline), against the random portfolios (gray).

The distribution shifted to the right, and the median strategy went from 11.4% a year to 17.4%. That put the middle of the distribution 2.4 points a year ahead of the S&P 500, and 65% of the strategies finished ahead of it on return.

Keep in mind that weighting about ten stocks by size put about half of the money into the largest company, so the portfolios were much less diversified and the spread went up. The middle 90% of the strategies went from 1.9% to 16.7% a year in equal amounts to 2.0% to 27.4% by size. Five of the strategies lost 5% a year or more, the worst 13.1%, and ten made 30% a year or more, the best 34.5%. That's quite a large distribution.

Return for each unit of risk

Each strategy's returns also moved around more from quarter to quarter. Volatility measures how much. It's the standard deviation of the quarterly returns, scaled up to a year.

Figure 11. Volatility over a year, and the median of each group.

The median strategy's volatility went from 16.5% in equal amounts to 22.9% by size, against 14.0% for the S&P 500 and 23.5% for the random portfolios in equal amounts.

One way to account for that is the Sharpe ratio: the average return per year minus the risk-free rate, divided by the volatility. The higher it is, the more return you got for each unit of risk. I set the risk-free rate to 0% in these tests.

Figure 12. The Sharpe ratio, and the median of each group.

Weighted by size, the median strategy's Sharpe ratio was 0.85, up from 0.71 in equal amounts and about twice random picking's 0.42. But the S&P 500's was 1.08. So the typical size-weighted strategy earned more than the index, but per unit of risk the S&P 500 still came out ahead, and only about one in five of the strategies (18%) beat it on that measure.

Thirty stocks instead of ten

The spread went up because weighting about ten stocks by size left each portfolio less diversified, and the simplest way to diversify a portfolio is to hold more stocks. So I ran the same test again with every strategy holding 30 stocks.

Figure 13. Return per year of the strategies weighted by size: 30 stocks each (green bars) and about 10 (dashed outline).

The distribution got narrower. The middle 90% went from 2.0% to 27.4% a year to 6.8% to 27.0%, and the median strategy's return went up to 18.4% a year. Volatility came down from 22.9% to 20.0%, and the Sharpe ratios improved a lot.

Figure 14. Sharpe ratio of the strategies weighted by size: 30 stocks each (green bars) and about 10 (dashed outline).

The median Sharpe ratio went from 0.85 to 1.00, slightly below the S&P 500's 1.08 over the same ten years. It went up for 76% of the strategies, and the share of strategies with a Sharpe ratio above the S&P 500's went from 18% to 35%.

I also ran the same test at 10, 20, 50 and 100 stocks.

Table 1. The 492 strategies weighted by company size, by how many stocks each one held, August 2016 to July 2026. Medians across the strategies, before trading costs and taxes. The first row is the sweep as it was run.

Volatility kept coming down as the strategies held more stocks, and the Sharpe ratio kept going up. At 100 stocks, the median strategy's Sharpe ratio was 1.14, and 61% of the strategies were above the S&P 500's 1.08. Return per year was highest around 30 stocks, at 18.4%, and came back to 17.4% at 100. With more stocks, the strategies also moved closer to the market as a whole: at 100 stocks the median strategy's volatility was 15.7%, against 14.0% for the S&P 500.

What I took from this

The strategy I opened with returned 25.2% a year for ten years, and it was picking stocks at random. It looked that good because I ran it 500 times and showed you the best one. The best result out of a set of backtests tells you how many backtests were run. It doesn't tell you how good the strategy is.

What actually tells you something is the full distribution of results: where its middle sits against random picks from the same list, how wide it is, because that width is the range you would have ended up somewhere inside, and whether both of those held on data the strategy never saw.

The S&P 500 is an example of that. A few rules on size and profit moved the middle of the distribution to the right, and holding its largest companies in the largest amounts raised its return above that middle. The strategies I built from company financial statements did the same kind of thing on a list of every US stock. Their middle sat above random picks from the same list, 11.4% a year against 7.3%, and the gap held in the two sealed years. Weighted by size, the typical strategy passed the S&P 500 on return, and holding 30 stocks instead of about 10 brought its return per unit of risk close to the index's.

So the next time you see a backtest, ask three questions:

  1. How many were run?
  2. Did it beat random picks with the same number of stocks, from the same list?
  3. What held up afterward?
